How to Grow Yellow Bells (Tecoma stans) in Your Desert Garden

October 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by Emilio Sánchez Hernández on Pexels

Walk through any established Sonoran Desert neighborhood in late summer and you'll notice one shrub above all others: a dense, rounded mound absolutely covered in clusters of golden trumpet flowers, alive with hummingbirds, bees, and butterflies. That's Tecoma stans — yellow bells — and it may be the single most rewarding flowering shrub you can plant in a desert garden.

Yellow bells blooms from late spring all the way through the first frost, often five to seven months of continuous color. It shrugs off 115°F heat that wilts lesser plants, tolerates alkaline caliche soils, and once established survives on monsoon rainfall alone in much of the Sonoran Desert. The Arizona native variety is cold-hardier than its tropical relatives, making it viable across the low and middle desert. If you're starting out and want one plant that delivers maximum impact for minimal effort, this is it — pair it with the fundamentals in our beginner's guide and you'll be set.

Tecoma stans: Two Varieties Worth Knowing

Nurseries sell two main forms, and the distinction matters enormously for desert gardeners:

VarietyCold HardinessMature SizeNotes
Arizona Yellow Bells (T. stans var. angustata)15–18°F (Zone 8b)4–6 ft tallNative to SE Arizona and W Texas; smaller, finer-leafed, more cold-tolerant
Mexican/Tropical Yellow Bells (T. stans var. stans)25–28°F (Zone 9b)8–12 ft tallBroader, showier, but dies to the ground in hard freezes

In Tucson, Phoenix, and the Chihuahuan Desert of west Texas, the Arizona variety is the safe choice. In Yuma, the lower Coachella Valley, and frost-free coastal areas, the larger Mexican form is spectacular. Both recover from frost damage by resprouting vigorously from the root crown — even the tropical variety often returns after a cold winter once the roots are established.

Where to Plant Yellow Bells

Sun is non-negotiable. Tecoma stans blooms most profusely in full sun — at least six hours of direct sun, and eight is better. In partial shade it grows but produces sparse, leggy growth and far fewer flowers. The more reflected heat it gets (south-facing walls, hot gravel mulch beds), the happier it is.

Soil drainage is the other critical factor. Yellow bells tolerates everything from sandy desert washes to rocky decomposed granite to heavy clay — but it will not tolerate prolonged waterlogged roots. On heavier soils, plant it in a raised area or amend the planting hole generously with decomposed granite to improve drainage. The same principle applies as with watering cacti and succulents: fast drainage between waterings is what prevents root rot.

Yellow bells planted against a south- or west-facing block wall — where heat radiates day and night — will bloom two to three weeks earlier in spring and last weeks longer into fall than the same plant in an open yard.

Allow plenty of room. The Arizona form reaches 4–6 feet tall and wide; the Mexican form can exceed 10 feet. Planted too close to walkways, it becomes a pruning burden and loses its naturally graceful, arching form.

When to Plant

The ideal planting window for yellow bells is spring after the last frost date — late February to April in the low desert, March to May at higher elevations. This gives the plant a full warm season to establish its root system before facing its first winter. Fall planting (September–October) works in the low desert, but give the plant at least six weeks before frost to get settled. Avoid planting in the dead of summer heat without a consistent watering plan, as new transplants can desiccate quickly. For more on timing your desert plantings, see our seasonal planting guide.

How to Plant Yellow Bells

  1. Dig the hole twice as wide as the container but no deeper. In clay soils, dig 2–3 inches shallower so the root ball sits slightly above the soil line.
  2. Amend sparingly. On sandy desert soil, no amendment is needed — just backfill with native soil. On clay or caliche, mix one part decomposed granite into two parts native soil.
  3. Remove and loosen. Remove the plant from its container and gently loosen any circling roots at the bottom.
  4. Set and backfill. Position the plant so the top of the root ball sits level with or slightly above the surrounding grade. Backfill, tamping gently as you go to eliminate air pockets.
  5. Water in deeply. Flood the planting basin slowly to settle the soil and push water down to the root zone. You want moisture at 18–24 inches depth.
  6. Mulch. Apply 3–4 inches of decomposed granite or shredded wood mulch over the root zone, keeping it a few inches away from the base of the plant.

Watering Yellow Bells

Yellow bells is drought-tolerant once established — but "once established" takes one full growing season, sometimes two. During the first year, water newly planted shrubs every 7–10 days in summer, letting the soil dry out between waterings. Deep, infrequent waterings train roots downward and build drought resilience far more effectively than shallow frequent watering.

After the first year in the low desert, most yellow bells survive on Sonoran monsoon rain alone from July through September. Before monsoon season, one or two deep waterings per month is sufficient. After establishment, supplemental irrigation is mainly needed in the hottest, driest periods — March through June — when rainfall is scant. In the Chihuahuan Desert, where monsoon rains are less reliable, bi-monthly watering in summer may be needed.

A single 1-gallon-per-hour drip emitter placed 12–18 inches from the base works well for supplemental watering. Avoid overhead irrigation on the foliage, which can encourage powdery mildew in humid monsoon conditions.

Fertilizing: Less Is More

In its native Sonoran habitat, Tecoma stans grows in nutrient-poor rocky soils. Too much nitrogen produces a flush of soft, lush foliage at the expense of flowers, and overfed plants are more vulnerable to frost. If you're planting in sandy or rocky desert soil, no fertilizer is needed. On heavier or organic-amended soils, one light application of a balanced low-nitrogen fertilizer in early spring is more than enough.

Pruning for Maximum Bloom

Yellow bells blooms on new growth, which means thoughtful pruning directly drives flower production. In the low desert, the best approach is a hard cutback in late winter (February) before new growth begins — cutting the plant back by one-third to one-half of its height. This encourages a flush of vigorous new growth that blooms prolifically from late spring through summer.

In colder zones (Zone 8b), frost often does the pruning for you — the plant may die back to the ground in a hard freeze, then resprout enthusiastically in spring. Resist the urge to cut back frost-burned stems until new growth has confirmed which wood is alive.

Light deadheading throughout the season isn't strictly necessary — yellow bells drops its spent blooms cleanly — but removing old flower clusters can stimulate a new flush faster and keeps the plant looking tidy.

Pests and Problems

Yellow Leaves

Yellowing leaves in desert-planted yellow bells usually point to overwatering or poor drainage, not nutrient deficiency. Before reaching for fertilizer, check soil moisture at 6–8 inches depth. If it's still damp from the last rain or irrigation, hold off on watering and let it dry.

Powdery Mildew

Monsoon humidity can trigger powdery mildew on crowded or shaded plants. The best prevention is good air circulation — don't plant yellow bells too close to walls or other dense shrubs. Established plants typically grow through minor mildew without lasting damage.

Caterpillars

Tecoma stans is the primary host plant for the Crimson Patch butterfly (Chlosyne janais) in Texas and northern Mexico. You may find clusters of orange caterpillars stripping leaves in summer — this is a feature, not a bug. The plant recovers quickly, and hosting native butterfly larvae is exactly what a wildlife garden should do.

Wildlife Value: A Hummingbird and Pollinator Magnet

Few desert shrubs rival yellow bells for pure wildlife attraction. The deep, tubular flowers are perfectly sized for hummingbird bills — both Anna's and Costa's hummingbirds work the plant obsessively from May through October. Native carpenter bees, queen bees, and digger bees are equally devoted, hovering into each bell to reach the nectar. Monarch and queen butterflies stop regularly as well.

If you're building a layered pollinator garden, yellow bells belongs in the mid-layer alongside companion plants like autumn sage (Salvia greggii), desert marigold (Baileya multiradiata), and penstemon. The combination of blooming periods extends hummingbird habitat from early spring through frost.

Companion Plants for Yellow Bells

Yellow bells pairs beautifully with other Sonoran Desert natives that share its heat and drought tolerance:

  • Ruellia peninsularis (Baja ruellia) — purple tubular flowers complement yellow bells' gold all summer
  • Salvia greggii (Autumn sage) — red and pink blooms extend the hummingbird season from fall into winter
  • Penstemon parryi (Parry's penstemon) — spring pink spikes precede yellow bells' summer show
  • Encelia farinosa (Brittlebush) — the silvery mounding foliage contrasts beautifully with yellow bells' deep green leaves
  • Palo verde or desert willow as a canopy tree — the filtered shade from a nurse tree can help yellow bells establish faster in the first summer

Yellow Bells in Containers

The Arizona variety grows well in large containers — 15-gallon minimum — making it usable on patios, courtyards, and rooftop gardens where in-ground planting isn't possible. Container plants need more frequent watering than in-ground plants (every 5–7 days in peak summer heat), and benefit from a slow-release fertilizer applied once in spring. Move containers against a protected wall before frost.

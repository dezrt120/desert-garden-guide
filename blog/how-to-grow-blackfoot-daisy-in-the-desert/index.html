How to Grow Blackfoot Daisy in the Desert

August 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by Nothing Ahead on Pexels

Ask any veteran Southwestern gardener to name a perennial that blooms nine months a year, needs almost no water, laughs at 110°F afternoons, and grows in the caliche pan behind the shed — and blackfoot daisy (Melampodium leucanthum) will come up more often than almost anything else. This tidy little Chihuahuan and Sonoran Desert native forms a low, silvery-green mound covered in small white daisies from March through November, and once it's established you can essentially forget it exists. It will keep blooming anyway.

The catch, if there is one, is that blackfoot daisy despises three things: rich soil, damp roots, and shade. Give it the opposite of what a nursery-catalog perennial wants and it will outlive most of your other plants. Here's how to site, plant, and grow it well.

Meet the plant

Blackfoot daisy is a woody-based perennial in the aster family, native to the rocky limestone slopes and gravelly flats of west Texas, southern New Mexico, southeastern Arizona, and northern Mexico. It matures at 6–12 inches tall and 12–18 inches wide, forming a rounded cushion of narrow gray-green leaves. The flowers are white, about an inch across, with a yellow center — small individually but produced by the hundreds. The common name comes from the tiny black bases of the seeds, not from anything on the plant you'd ever notice.

In the wild it's most abundant on caliche-rich alkaline soils that would kill a lawn. In a cultivated garden it fits neatly into rock gardens, front-yard xeriscapes, parking-strip plantings, and the front edge of any water-wise border. It pairs beautifully with damianita, desert marigold, penstemon, and low salvias — see our guide to desert companion planting for more combinations that share its cultural needs.

Blackfoot daisy is the plant you point at when a neighbor says nothing blooms in the desert without a hose.

Where it thrives

Blackfoot daisy is hardy from USDA zones 5 through 10, which covers essentially every low, middle, and high desert climate in the Southwest. It handles winter lows into the single digits without complaint and shrugs off Sonoran and Chihuahuan summers as long as the soil around its crown stays dry. Reflected heat off south-facing walls, driveways, and rock mulch doesn't bother it. Coastal humidity and heavy clay do.

Site selection

Sun and drainage — in that order — are the two variables that decide whether your blackfoot daisy lives for eight years or eight months.

Sun

Full sun, all day, every day. Six hours minimum, eight to ten is better. In half-day shade the plant survives but sprawls open, blooms sparsely, and rots at the crown during the first monsoon storm of the season. Save the east-side flowerbed and the filtered light under a mesquite canopy for something else — see our roundup of shade plants for desert gardens for those spots.

Drainage

Blackfoot daisy needs sharply drained, gravelly, alkaline soil. If you can push a hose into the ground and water runs off instead of soaking in, you've found the right spot. In heavier soils — including the compacted clay-loam of most suburban yards — plant it on a raised berm 6–12 inches above grade, or in a rock garden mounded with decomposed granite. Never plant it in a low spot where rainwater collects.

If you're battling caliche or hardpan, see our guide to dealing with caliche soil. The short version for blackfoot daisy: don't break through the caliche layer. Blackfoot daisy actually prefers alkaline, mineral soil — you just need enough loose depth (about 8 inches) for the root ball to establish before it hits the pan.

Planting

Fall is the ideal planting window — October through early December in most of the Southwest — because cool soil and mild days let roots establish before summer heat arrives. Early spring (February–March) is the second-best window. Avoid planting from May through September; the establishment watering required in summer heat often rots the crown of a plant that hasn't yet anchored itself. Our full breakdown of the best time of year to plant in the desert covers why timing matters so much for natives like this one.

Step by step

  1. Dig a hole twice as wide as the nursery pot but no deeper. The crown of the plant must sit slightly above finished grade — an inch high is fine.
  2. Do not amend the backfill. No compost, no potting soil, no fertilizer. Native mineral soil is exactly what this plant evolved to grow in.
  3. Backfill halfway, water in gently to settle, then finish backfilling. Do not tamp hard.
  4. Top-dress with a 1–2 inch layer of decomposed granite or 3/8-inch gravel. Keep the mulch pulled back an inch from the crown. Never use wood chips or bark mulch on this plant — they hold moisture around the stem and cause rot.
  5. Water deeply once at planting and again three days later.

A good pair of felco-style bypass pruners and a solid stainless-steel hand trowel are all the tools you need. If you're planting into caliche, keep a heavy pick or digging bar nearby — a shovel alone will not get you through it.

Watering

This is where most blackfoot daisies die. Gardeners see the tiny plant, the summer heat, and the drip emitter — and they run the drip too long. The plant then greens up, blooms briefly, and collapses at the crown a few weeks later.

Here's a realistic first-year schedule for a fall-planted specimen in the low desert:

SeasonFrequencyAmount
First 4 weeks (fall)Every 5–7 days1 gallon, slow soak
Weeks 5–12 (fall/winter)Every 10–14 days1 gallon
Spring of year 1Every 10 days1–2 gallons
Summer of year 1Every 7–10 days1 gallon at dawn
Year 2 and beyondEvery 3–4 weeks, or rainfall only2 gallons

Once established, blackfoot daisy in the ground rarely needs supplemental water outside of an unusually dry summer. In monsoon country, natural rainfall is often enough for the plant's entire lifespan. If you're running a drip system, use a single 1-gph emitter placed 4–6 inches from the crown, not against it. Ongoing care

Pruning and deadheading

Blackfoot daisy is largely self-cleaning — old flowers drop on their own — so deadheading is optional. What matters is a hard cutback once a year to keep the mound tight and prevent the woody base from getting leggy. In late winter, before new growth breaks, shear the entire plant back by half with a clean pair of bypass pruning shears. It looks brutal for two weeks. It will be twice as full in six.

Fertilizing

Skip it. Fertilizer produces soft, floppy growth that flops open and rots. If the plant looks pale, the fix is almost never nitrogen — it's usually too much water.

Pests and disease

Almost none. Deer and rabbits generally leave it alone. The only real threat is root and crown rot from overwatering or from wood-based mulch trapping humidity. If a plant collapses suddenly at the crown, pull it and check your emitter placement or drainage before replanting.

Design ideas

Because blackfoot daisy stays low, tight, and continuously in bloom, it works in places most perennials can't: the sun-baked strip between the sidewalk and the driveway, the front edge of a raised bed against a south wall, the gaps between flagstones on a decomposed-granite path. Plant it in drifts of three, five, or seven for the strongest visual effect — a single plant tends to disappear against a large landscape.

Pair it with the deep purple spikes of Parry's penstemon (Penstemon parryi), the yellow of desert marigold (Baileya multiradiata), the orange of globe mallow (Sphaeralcea ambigua), and the airy pink of Muhly grass (Muhlenbergia capillaris). A packet of native Southwestern wildflower seed broadcast in fall alongside a few gallon-sized blackfoot daisies gives you an instant, self-seeding meadow.

Propagation

Blackfoot daisy self-seeds readily in bare, gravelly soil — you'll often find volunteer seedlings tucked between rocks or at the edge of a decomposed-granite path a year or two after planting. Transplant them small (before the taproot exceeds three inches) and they take reliably. Seed sown directly in fall germinates with winter rains; expect roughly 30–50% germination on fresh seed.

Softwood cuttings taken in late spring root in about three weeks in coarse perlite kept barely damp, but honestly, the plant is inexpensive enough at any Southwestern nursery that most gardeners skip propagation and just buy another gallon pot.

The bottom line

Blackfoot daisy asks for full sun, sharp drainage, and to be left alone. In exchange it gives you nine months of white flowers a year, decade-long lifespan, and the kind of easy reliability that makes desert gardening feel possible. If you're building a low-water front yard or filling a rock garden, put three plants together, water them through the first fall, and then get out of the way.

How to Use Ollas in Your Desert Garden: Ancient Irrigation That Still Works

October 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by Ylanite Koppens on Pexels

Ollas (pronounced "OY-yahs") are one of the oldest irrigation tools on Earth — unglazed clay pots buried in the soil, filled with water, and left to slowly seep moisture directly into the root zone of surrounding plants. The technique is at least 4,000 years old, developed independently by farmers in China, sub-Saharan Africa, and the desert cultures of the Americas. Today, desert gardeners from Tucson to Phoenix are rediscovering what those ancient growers already knew: for plants in arid soil, nothing delivers water more efficiently.

If you've been fighting evaporation losses, runoff, and overwatering, ollas offer a radically different approach — one that lets the soil and the plants themselves regulate how much water they receive.

How an Olla Works

The science behind ollas is elegant. An unglazed terracotta vessel is porous enough that water molecules can seep through its walls at a very slow rate. When the olla is buried in dry soil, the difference in water potential between the vessel and the surrounding earth creates capillary suction that draws water outward. As the soil in the root zone becomes moist and that suction decreases, the seepage naturally slows. When roots dry out the surrounding soil again, seepage picks back up.

The result is genuine on-demand irrigation — not a timer approximation of it. A well-placed olla doesn't know what time it is. It responds to actual soil moisture conditions and actual plant demand in real time.

Why Ollas Are Perfect for Desert Conditions

In the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts, standard surface irrigation loses an enormous percentage of applied water to evaporation before it ever reaches plant roots — studies estimate 30 to 50 percent or more during summer. Sprinklers and even surface drip emitters are fighting physics every time the sun is out.

Ollas sidestep this problem entirely. Because water is delivered below the soil surface, directly into the root zone, there is almost no evaporative loss. Weed seeds on the surface stay dry and ungerminated. Deep root growth is encouraged because moisture consistently exists at depth rather than at the surface. And water that would otherwise run off impermeable desert caliche stays in the soil where it belongs.

Research from New Mexico State University found that olla irrigation used 50 to 70 percent less water than conventional surface irrigation for the same yield in vegetable trials. In a climate where water costs money and conservation is a civic responsibility, those numbers matter.

"An olla doesn't fight the desert — it works with it. The soil itself becomes the irrigation controller."

If you're already using drip irrigation, ollas can complement or partially replace it, especially in vegetable beds and containers where precise root-zone delivery pays the biggest dividends.

Choosing the Right Olla for Your Garden

Commercial ollas are available in sizes ranging from 1 quart to 5 gallons. The right size depends on what you're planting:

  • 1–2 quart: Container gardens, herb pots, small transplants
  • 1 gallon: Single large container plants, raised bed herbs and vegetables
  • 2–3 gallon: In-ground vegetable beds, perennial flowers, young desert shrubs
  • 5 gallon: Large vegetable beds, established perennials, small native trees

Look for unglazed clay ollas with a narrow neck (to reduce evaporation from the opening) and no internal glaze. The exterior must be fully unglazed — even a partial glaze dramatically reduces seepage. Inspect for hairline cracks before purchase; a cracked olla will seep unevenly or drain too fast.

Installing Ollas in Your Garden: Step by Step

Installation is straightforward, but placement and depth matter.

1. Dig the hole before planting

The best time to install an olla is before you set out transplants. Dig a hole slightly larger than the olla's body, deep enough so only the neck and opening sit at or just above the soil surface. If you're retrofitting an established bed, dig carefully to avoid damaging existing roots.

2. Set the olla and backfill

Place the olla in the hole and firm soil around it, removing air pockets that would disrupt capillary flow. The neck should protrude just enough to fill easily — about one to two inches above grade.

3. Space ollas for full coverage

A 2- to 3-gallon olla moistens soil in roughly an 18- to 24-inch radius around its body. For vegetable beds, space ollas 24 to 30 inches apart in a grid pattern. For individual desert perennials, one olla per plant is sufficient for establishment; mature established plants typically don't need ollas at all.

4. Cover the opening

Always cap or cover the olla neck when not filling. A small flat stone, a piece of mesh, or a fitted terracotta lid prevents evaporation from the opening, deters mosquitoes from breeding, and keeps debris out. Without a cover, you'll lose a surprisingly large fraction of your stored water directly to the dry desert air.

5. Fill regularly

Check ollas every two to four days in summer (daily in peak July–August heat), weekly in cooler months. Fill to the neck, cap, and walk away. During monsoon season, ollas may not need refilling for days at a time — the surrounding moist soil reduces their seepage rate automatically.

What to Plant Around Ollas

Ollas reward any plant with a reasonably dense root system, but they shine in these desert applications:

  • Vegetables and herbs: Tomatoes, peppers, squash, basil, and cilantro respond dramatically to olla irrigation in desert vegetable gardens. Root vegetables like carrots and beets benefit from deep, consistent moisture.
  • Native perennials during establishment: Newly planted desert willow (Chilopsis linearis), globe mallow (Sphaeralcea ambigua), and desert marigold (Baileya multiradiata) establish faster when an olla keeps the root zone consistently moist through their first summer.
  • Container plants: A 1-quart olla in a large container pot can eliminate daily watering in all but the most extreme heat.

DIY Ollas: Making Your Own from Terra Cotta Pots

Commercial ollas can be pricey — $15 to $40 each depending on size. A functional DIY version uses two unglazed terra cotta pots of the same size:

  1. Plug the drainage hole of one pot with a waterproof adhesive or two-part epoxy putty. Allow to cure fully.
  2. Apply a bead of food-safe silicone sealant around the rim of the sealed pot.
  3. Press the second pot (drainage hole facing up to serve as the filling port) firmly onto the first, creating a sealed chamber.
  4. Allow 24 hours to cure, then test by filling with water on a hard surface. A slow weep from the sides is correct; any flow from the sealed bottom indicates a leak to re-seal.

DIY ollas work well for vegetable beds and are a fraction of the cost of commercial versions. The seepage rate is higher than purpose-built ollas because standard pots aren't fired for the same porosity, but they still dramatically outperform surface drip on water efficiency.

Seasonal Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Ollas are nearly maintenance-free, but a few seasonal tasks extend their life.

Before winter

In areas that experience freezing temperatures — including high-elevation desert gardens in Albuquerque, Flagstaff, and the Chihuahuan plateau — drain and remove ollas before the first hard freeze. Water expands when it freezes and will crack even a quality clay vessel. In low-desert zones like the Phoenix metro and lower Tucson valley, ollas can typically stay in the ground year-round.

Mineral and algae buildup

Desert water is often high in calcium and magnesium carbonates. White mineral crusting on olla walls can partially clog pores over time. Soak affected ollas in a 50/50 solution of white vinegar and water for 24 hours, then scrub with a stiff brush. If green algae grows inside the reservoir, add a splash of white vinegar to the water; it won't harm plants and keeps algae suppressed without chemicals.

Monitoring soil moisture

During the first season, use a soil moisture meter to verify that water is reaching the root zone of your target plants. If the soil 12 inches from the olla stays dry, the olla may be too small for the soil type or the planting density may exceed its capacity.

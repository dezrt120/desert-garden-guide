The Best Desert Plants for Attracting Butterflies

August 2026  ·  6 min read

Photo by Seng Lam Ho on Pexels

A desert yard teeming with painted ladies, giant swallowtails, and queen butterflies isn't an accident — it's the result of putting the right plants in the right places. The good news for arid-climate gardeners is that many of the Southwest's best butterfly magnets are drought-tolerant natives that practically take care of themselves once established. You don't need a water-hungry flower bed to attract these pollinators; you need to understand what butterflies actually look for.

Butterflies seek two distinct things from your garden: nectar sources for adult feeding and host plants where females lay eggs and caterpillars feed. A garden stocked with only nectar plants will draw visitors, but one that also includes host plants becomes a full butterfly habitat — a place where generations of insects are born and raised. The plants below deliver on both fronts, and every one of them thrives in the Sonoran, Chihuahuan, and Mojave deserts with minimal supplemental water.

Top Nectar Plants for Desert Butterflies

Lantana (Lantana camara and native species)

If you plant only one butterfly-attracting species, make it lantana. The flower clusters — each a tight bouquet of orange, yellow, red, and pink — are like a landing pad designed by butterflies. The multi-tube florets are the perfect depth for butterfly proboscises, and a single established shrub can host dozens of painted ladies on a warm September afternoon. Desert-adapted varieties tolerate 110°F heat and bloom from spring through first frost with just monthly deep watering. For a full planting guide, see our post on how to grow lantana in the desert.

Desert Milkweed (Asclepias subulata, A. linaria, and A. erosa)

Milkweed is monarch territory, but the native desert species also host queen butterflies (Danaus gilippus), a close relative of the monarch that's far more common in the low desert year-round. Desert milkweed species produce clusters of cream or white flowers and grow as upright, nearly leafless stems that store water efficiently. Asclepias subulata (rush milkweed) is especially heat-tough and can survive on rainfall alone in the Sonoran Desert. It's both nectar source and sole caterpillar host plant for monarchs and queens — one plant does double duty. Our guide on growing milkweed for monarchs in the desert covers species selection and care in detail.

Globe Mallow (Sphaeralcea ambigua)

Few desert wildflowers are as generous as globe mallow — apricot, coral, red, and white blooms covering knee-high mounds from late winter through early summer, and again after monsoon rains. It's a native of rocky washes and roadsides across the Sonoran and Mojave deserts, which means it evolved to thrive on negligible water. Painted ladies, checkered skippers, and common checkered-skippers all use globe mallow as both a nectar source and larval host plant. Scatter globe mallow seeds into a prepared bed after the first fall rains and expect blooms by late February in the low desert.

Desert Marigold (Baileya multiradiata)

Desert marigold is one of the few plants that blooms nearly year-round in warm desert climates — waves of bright yellow daisy-like flowers on silvery stems, attractive to sulphur butterflies, skippers, and cloudless sulphurs in particular. It self-seeds readily and forms colonies once established. Plant it in full sun, in well-drained soil or decomposed granite, and give it virtually nothing after its first season.

"A butterfly garden in the desert doesn't fight the climate — it works with it. Native nectar plants evolved alongside these pollinators for thousands of years, and that relationship shows every time a swallowtail lands."

Autumn Sage (Salvia greggii)

Native to the Chihuahuan Desert and West Texas foothills, autumn sage earns its keep by blooming in shades of red, pink, coral, and white from spring all the way through fall — exactly when migrating and resident butterflies need fuel. Giant swallowtails are particularly fond of it. Its tubular flowers also draw hummingbirds, so it's pulling double pollinator duty in your yard. Plant it in full sun or light afternoon shade in USDA zones 7–10.

Host Plants That Complete the Butterfly Life Cycle

Pipevine (Aristolochia watsonii and A. californica)

The pipevine swallowtail (Battus philenor) — with its iridescent blue-green hindwings — is one of the most striking butterflies in the Southwest. Its caterpillars feed exclusively on pipevine (Aristolochia species), and the plant is the single reason these butterflies appear in your neighborhood. Desert pipevine (A. watsonii) is a small ground-hugging vine native to the Sonoran Desert, easy to grow in partial shade, and available at specialty native plant nurseries. Once you plant it, swallowtails will find it.

Senna / Cassia (Senna artemisioides and relatives)

The cloudless sulphur butterfly (Phoebis sennae) and sleepy orange (Abaeis nicippe) both rely on senna species as larval hosts. These tough, silvery-leaved desert shrubs produce cascades of bright yellow flowers in early spring and again in fall, and they require almost no supplemental water once established in the low desert. They're a perfect companion to globe mallow and desert marigold in a naturalistic butterfly border, and they make great companion plants for other drought-tolerant species.

Supporting Your Butterfly Habitat

Add a Puddling Station

Male butterflies "puddle" — gathering at damp soil to absorb salts and minerals. A shallow butterfly puddling dish filled with damp sand and a pinch of sea salt near your nectar plants dramatically increases butterfly activity. Place it in a sunny, open spot and refresh the moisture every couple of days — desert heat evaporates it quickly.

Timing Your Planting

Fall is the ideal planting window for most butterfly-attracting natives in the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts — roots establish during cooler months and the plants hit spring butterfly season fully settled. Our guide to the best time of year to plant in the desert walks through the seasonal calendar in detail. Spring planting works too, provided you can keep transplants alive through their first hot season with regular deep watering.

Reduce or Eliminate Pesticides

Even organic pesticides like spinosad can harm butterfly caterpillars. In your butterfly zone, tolerate some chewing on host plants — that's the habitat working as intended. If pest pressure on other plants is a concern, targeted applications well away from host plants are your safest option. Letting caterpillars do their thing is non-negotiable if you want butterflies to complete their life cycle in your yard.

Create Shelter and Overwintering Spots

Shrubby native plants like desert lavender (Hyptis emoryi), brittlebush (Encelia farinosa), and fairy duster (Calliandra eriophylla) give adult butterflies shelter from wind and a place to roost overnight. A butterfly house tucked among shrubs gives them a dedicated spot in your garden. Leave some fallen leaves and twigs at the base of shrubs — many species overwinter as chrysalides attached to stems just inches from the ground.

Building Your Desert Butterfly Garden

The most effective butterfly gardens layer nectar plants of different heights and bloom times so there's always something in flower. Start with one or two anchor shrubs — lantana and autumn sage are the easiest starting point — then add a groundcover like globe mallow and a host vine like pipevine. You'll see results within one season. For broader design inspiration on how to layer desert plants effectively, our companion planting guide covers which species work well together in the same bed.

The desert's butterfly community is richer than most people expect — over 250 species have been recorded in Arizona alone. With the right plants and a pesticide-free approach, you can turn even a small yard into a genuine wildlife corridor. Start with three or four of the plants above, watch what arrives, and let the garden evolve from there.

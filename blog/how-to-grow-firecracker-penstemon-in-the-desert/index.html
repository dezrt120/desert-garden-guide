How to Grow Firecracker Penstemon in the Desert

August 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by David Desrocher on Pexels

Most desert gardens go quiet in November. Firecracker penstemon (Penstemon eatonii) does the opposite — it shoots up tall wands of scarlet-red tubular flowers right when everything else has retreated for winter, turning your yard into a hummingbird magnet from December through April. It is one of the most spectacular native wildflowers you can grow in the Sonoran or Mojave Desert, and one of the most underused.

If you want winter color without irrigation drama, this is the plant. Here is exactly how to choose the right site, plant it at the right time of year, and keep it thriving for years.

What Is Firecracker Penstemon?

Penstemon eatonii — named for 19th-century botanist Daniel Coit Eaton — is a clump-forming herbaceous perennial native to the Mojave Desert, Sonoran Desert foothills, and high-desert scrub of Arizona, Utah, Nevada, and Southern California. It grows 2–4 feet tall, forming a basal rosette of bright green, lance-shaped leaves that stay evergreen through mild winters. In late fall or early winter, stiff upright flower stalks emerge, each carrying dozens of scarlet to coral-red tubular blooms that are almost exclusively pollinated by hummingbirds.

It is not the same plant as Penstemon superbus (superb penstemon) or Penstemon parryi (Parry's penstemon), though all three share that signature hummingbird-magnet shape. Firecracker penstemon stands out for its intense scarlet color and its preference for lower elevations and drier conditions.

"Firecracker penstemon is the plant I recommend first to anyone who wants to attract hummingbirds to a low-water Sonoran garden. It blooms in winter when virtually nothing else does — and the birds go absolutely wild for it."

The Right Conditions

Sunlight

Full sun is non-negotiable. Firecracker penstemon needs at least six hours of direct sun daily and blooms best with eight or more. In partial shade it produces fewer flower stalks, stretches toward the light, and becomes susceptible to fungal problems. Choose your sunniest, most open spot.

Soil

This plant is built for lean, fast-draining desert soils. It handles alkaline caliche and rocky decomposed granite beautifully — the same conditions that would rot a garden center perennial in a season. What it cannot tolerate is wet feet. Heavy clay or soil that holds moisture longer than 24 hours after rain will rot the crown and kill the plant, usually within the first summer. If your soil is dense, amend the planting area generously with coarse sand and gravel, or plant in a raised berm. Learn how to evaluate your desert soil before planting — it is the single most important step for long-term success with plants like this one.

Temperature

Firecracker penstemon is cold-hardy to about 0°F (USDA zones 5–10), which makes it an excellent choice for high-desert gardens in Arizona and New Mexico where temperatures occasionally dip below freezing. In the low Sonoran Desert (Phoenix, Tucson), it sails through winter without protection. Summer heat — even 115°F in the shade — does not kill it, though it goes semi-dormant during the hottest months and looks its best in the cooler seasons.

When and How to Plant

The ideal planting window in the low Sonoran Desert is October through February. This cool-season establishment period lets the plant develop its root system before summer heat arrives, dramatically increasing survival rates. In the Mojave or high desert, plant in early spring once the danger of hard frost has passed — usually March to April.

You can start from seed or transplant from nursery containers. Both work well, but container-grown plants give you a one- to two-year head start on bloom time.

Planting from Containers

  1. Dig a hole twice as wide as the rootball and the same depth — no deeper.
  2. Mix the native soil with about 30% coarse gravel or decomposed granite to improve drainage.
  3. Set the plant at the same level it grew in the pot — never sink the crown below grade.
  4. Water deeply at planting, then once a week for the first four to six weeks.
  5. After establishment, back off to once every two to three weeks in summer, and let winter rains handle the rest.

Planting from Seed

Seeds germinate readily with a period of cold stratification — 30 days in a damp paper towel in the refrigerator works well. Sow stratified seeds in fall directly in prepared beds, or start indoors in late winter under full-spectrum grow lights. Transplant seedlings outdoors when they reach 3–4 inches tall. Expect blooms in the second year from seed.

Watering After Establishment

Once established — typically after one full year in the ground — firecracker penstemon is remarkably drought-tolerant. In the low Sonoran Desert it typically needs supplemental water only during the hottest, driest stretch (May–June), roughly once every two to three weeks. Winter rains and monsoon moisture usually provide everything else it needs.

The single most common killer of penstemon in desert gardens is overwatering. If your irrigation schedule runs on a fixed timer regardless of rainfall, put penstemon on a separate zone or hand-water it only when the soil is completely dry 2 inches down. Crown rot from wet soil is irreversible — the plant will look healthy one week and collapse the next.

Use a soil moisture meter if you are unsure about watering frequency — it removes the guesswork from drip irrigation scheduling.

Pruning and Maintenance

After the blooms fade — typically in April or May in the low desert — cut the flower stalks back to the base of the plant with clean pruners. Leave the basal rosette of leaves intact. The plant will rest through the summer heat, looking a bit ragged but alive, then perk up again in fall as temperatures drop and begin pushing new growth ahead of the next bloom season.

Every three to four years, clumps can become crowded and bloom less vigorously. Divide them in fall by lifting the entire clump, separating it into 3–4 rooted sections with a sharp spade, and replanting the divisions. This reinvigorates the plant and gives you free starts for other parts of the garden.

Deadheading individual spent blooms is not necessary — the plant will continue producing flowers even if you leave the old ones on — but removing stalks after bloom is finished does keep the plant looking tidy and redirects energy back to root and leaf growth.

Pests, Diseases, and Common Problems

Firecracker penstemon is remarkably trouble-free when planted in the right conditions. The problems that do occur almost always trace back to soil drainage or overwatering.

  • Crown rot: Caused by consistently wet soil. No cure once established — improve drainage before planting, not after.
  • Powdery mildew: Can appear on leaves during humid monsoon periods, especially in dense plantings with poor airflow. Space plants at least 18–24 inches apart. Rarely fatal.
  • Aphids: Occasionally appear on new growth in spring but are usually controlled quickly by the ladybugs and parasitic wasps that follow the hummingbirds into the garden. A strong blast of water from the hose handles small infestations.
  • Failure to bloom: Almost always caused by too much shade, too much water, or planting in heavy soil. Correct the underlying condition and the plant typically recovers.

A low-phosphorus native plant fertilizer applied lightly in fall can support root development before the bloom season, but established plants in lean desert soil generally need no fertilizer at all.

Attracting Hummingbirds

The long, narrow, scarlet tubes of Penstemon eatonii are essentially custom-built for hummingbird bills — Anna's hummingbirds and Costa's hummingbirds, which overwinter in the low desert, are the primary pollinators. Plant firecracker penstemon in groups of three or more to create a visible mass of color that migrating and resident hummingbirds can spot from a distance. Position the group near a window or patio where you can watch the action — in a good year, a single large clump can host a dozen visits an hour at peak bloom.

Pair it with other hummingbird plants for a multi-season feeding station: desert willow (Chilopsis linearis) blooms from May through August, bridging the gap between penstemon's spring finish and the monsoon-season bloomers like hummingbird mint.

Where to Buy Firecracker Penstemon

Look for Penstemon eatonii at native plant nurseries in Arizona, Nevada, and Southern California — it is rarely stocked at big-box garden centers, which tend to carry only conventional garden perennials. The Arizona Native Plant Society maintains a list of nurseries carrying local ecotype natives. Local ecotypes — plants grown from seed collected in your region — are better adapted to local soil and climate conditions than plants grown from out-of-state seed, so ask specifically for Sonoran or Mojave provenance when you can.

Seeds are available from several reputable native seed suppliers online. Starting from locally sourced seed is the most ecologically sound approach and connects your garden to the broader regional plant community.

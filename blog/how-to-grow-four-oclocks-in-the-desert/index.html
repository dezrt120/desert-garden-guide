How to Grow Four O'Clocks in the Desert

October 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by Mustafa Akın on Pexels

Few plants in the Sonoran, Chihuahuan, or Mojave desert reward so generously for so little effort. Four o'clocks (Mirabilis spp.) open their trumpet-shaped flowers every afternoon as the desert cools, flooding the garden with a subtle honey fragrance that draws hawk moths, hummingbirds, and white-lined sphinx moths from surprising distances. By morning the flowers close again — and the cycle repeats from late spring through the first hard frost.

Beneath the surface, the real secret to their toughness: an enormous woody taproot that can weigh 20 to 40 pounds in a mature plant, storing water and energy through drought and winter alike. This underground architecture makes four o'clocks a near-perfect desert perennial — they survive months of neglect and return more vigorously every year.

The Two Desert Four O'Clocks

Mirabilis multiflora — The True Desert Native

Desert four o'clock (Mirabilis multiflora) is native to the rocky desert grasslands and dry foothills of Arizona, New Mexico, and west Texas — the heart of the Chihuahuan Desert and the transition zones of the Sonoran. It grows as a low, spreading mound 1 to 2 feet tall and 3 to 4 feet wide, producing dense clusters of bright magenta-pink flowers all summer and into fall. This is the species best suited to naturalistic, unirrigated desert plantings: it can survive on as little as 8 to 10 inches of annual rainfall once established.

Mirabilis jalapa — The Garden Form

Common four o'clock (Mirabilis jalapa) originates from the Andes but has naturalized throughout the American Southwest. It grows 2 to 4 feet tall and produces flowers in a wider color range — white, yellow, magenta, coral, and bicolored. It performs exceptionally well in desert conditions and self-seeds prolifically after the first season, behaving as a naturalized perennial in frost-free zones (USDA 9b–11). Both species are suitable for Sonoran Desert gardens; M. multiflora is the better choice for water-free Chihuahuan and Mojave plantings.

Choosing a Location

Full sun is the non-negotiable requirement for both species — six or more hours of direct sun produces the most prolific blooming and keeps plants compact. M. jalapa tolerates a few hours of afternoon shade in the hottest parts of the low Sonoran Desert (Zone 9b–11), but shade reduces flower production dramatically. M. multiflora grows best in full, unobstructed desert sun and thrives in the reflected heat of south- and west-facing exposures.

Excellent drainage is equally essential. Never plant four o'clocks in low spots, clay basins, or areas that pond after monsoon storms. Their large, fleshy roots will rot in waterlogged soil within days. Plant them in raised beds, on slopes, or where the soil drains completely within a few hours of rain. How to Plant Four O'Clocks in the Desert

Starting from Seed

Four o'clock seeds are large, black, and easy to handle — similar in size to a small pea. They germinate reliably once soil temperatures reach 65°F: mid-March in the low Sonoran Desert (Phoenix, Tucson), late April in Chihuahuan elevations above 3,500 feet, and May in the Mojave.

Sow directly in the garden, 1/2 inch deep, spaced 18 to 24 inches apart. Plants spread considerably wider than their initial footprint, so resist the urge to crowd them. Germination takes 7 to 14 days with consistent light moisture. Once seedlings reach 3 inches tall, reduce watering to every 5 to 7 days to begin building drought tolerance. Timing your sowing for the optimal spring window is covered in detail in the desert planting calendar — early spring sowings catch the longest possible growing season before winter.

Planting Tubers

Mature four o'clock tubers are available from specialty native plant nurseries in late winter and early spring. Plant them 4 to 6 inches deep in well-drained, loosened soil. Tubers carry enough stored energy to push rapid early-season growth — plants from tubers typically bloom 4 to 6 weeks earlier than seedlings. Add a layer of coarse horticultural grit or perlite at the tuber's depth to improve drainage, especially in soils with any clay content.

Watering Four O'Clocks Through the Desert Year

Young plants need consistent moisture for the first 4 to 6 weeks while establishing their root system. Water deeply — saturate the top 12 to 18 inches of soil — twice a week until the plant shows vigorous new growth. Then reduce to once a week through spring. By July, monsoon rainfall in the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts typically meets or exceeds the plant's needs. If monsoons are sparse, supplement with one deep soak every 10 to 14 days.

The same deep, infrequent rhythm used for watering cacti and succulents applies here: saturate the root zone fully, then wait for the soil to dry almost completely before watering again. Shallow, frequent irrigation keeps the roots near the surface and increases susceptibility to summer heat stress and root rot. Established four o'clocks in the Sonoran Desert frequently thrive on monsoon rain alone from July through September, with no supplemental irrigation required.

Pruning, Deadheading, and Winter Care

Deadheading spent flowers — snipping the withered tubes — prolongs bloom by redirecting energy to new buds rather than seed production. If you allow seed heads to mature, four o'clocks self-seed readily; expect small colonies to naturalize in adjacent areas over several seasons, which most gardeners consider a bonus.

After the first hard frost, the top growth dies back to the ground. Leave the dead stems through winter — they mark the location of the tuber and provide a modest buffer against freezing temperatures. Cut them down in late February or early March, just before the root breaks dormancy and sends up new shoots.

In Zone 8 and colder, mulch the root zone with 4 to 6 inches of straw mulch or decomposed granite before the first forecast freeze. The tuber itself can survive temperatures down to 5°F (–15°C) with adequate mulch protection. In the low Sonoran Desert (Phoenix, Yuma, Tucson), where freezes are brief and moderate, no mulching is needed.

Wildlife and Pollinator Value

The long floral tubes of four o'clocks are sized precisely for hawk moths and white-lined sphinx moths (Hyles lineata), which hover at the flowers at dusk like small hummingbirds, feeding and pollinating in the same movement. Hummingbirds — particularly the broad-tailed and black-chinned species common to the Chihuahuan Desert — visit in late afternoon as the first blooms open. Once seed heads form in fall, they attract quail, doves, and small sparrows.

In a desert pollinator planting, four o'clocks fill a niche that few other plants cover: evening and nighttime nectar production. Most desert wildflowers close by afternoon, leaving the pollinator corridor dark after 5 PM. Four o'clocks bridge that gap from late afternoon through the following morning.

Four O'Clocks vs. Other Desert Wildflowers

Mirabilis multiflora is unusually long-lived for a desert wildflower — a single plant can persist for 10 to 20 years in the right spot, growing its taproot deeper and wider each season and becoming more drought-tolerant with age. Compare this to globe mallow or brittlebush, which peak at 3 to 5 years and then need replacement. The up-front establishment investment in a four o'clock pays dividends for decades.

Four o'clocks also spread more slowly than many self-seeding desert natives: they don't aggressively crowd out neighbors the way desert marigold or globe mallow can in moist years. This makes them well-suited to mixed plantings alongside native grasses, agave, and low-water perennials.

Whether you're building a hummingbird border, a night-blooming garden for evening patios, or simply want a no-fuss perennial that comes back stronger every year, four o'clocks deliver.

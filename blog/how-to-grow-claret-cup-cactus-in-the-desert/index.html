How to Grow Claret Cup Cactus (Echinocereus triglochidiatus) in the Desert

September 2026  ·  6 min read

Photo by Castorly Stock on Pexels

Every spring across the Sonoran, Chihuahuan, and Mojave deserts, rocky hillsides and canyon walls come alive with one of the most electrifying displays in North American botany: the blazing scarlet flowers of the claret cup cactus (Echinocereus triglochidiatus). Clusters of cylindrical stems — sometimes hundreds packed together — erupt with cup-shaped crimson blooms that draw hummingbirds from miles away. For desert gardeners, this native cactus delivers one of the most dramatic spring color shows imaginable with almost none of the effort.

What Is Claret Cup Cactus?

Claret cup (Echinocereus triglochidiatus), sometimes called king's cup or hedgehog cactus, is a clustering cactus native to the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. It grows in rocky desert terrain from sea level up to 7,000 feet elevation, making it remarkably cold-hardy for a cactus. Mature plants form dense mounds of ribbed cylindrical stems, ranging from a handful of stems to clumps of 500 or more in old specimens.

The blooms are the main event: large, waxy, cup-shaped flowers in vivid scarlet or orange-red appear from March through June depending on elevation and location. Each flower opens during the day and closes at night, and individual blooms last five to seven days. Flowers are followed by small, edible red fruits with a mild, sweet flavor.

Several notable varieties exist across the Southwest:

VarietyRangeNotes
var. triglochidiatusSonoran & Chihuahuan desertsMost common; moderate spines
var. mojavensisMojave DesertMore spines, larger stems
var. melanacanthusUtah, Nevada, ColoradoDark spines, high cold tolerance

Why Grow Claret Cup?

Beyond sheer beauty, claret cup earns its place in any desert garden through exceptional resilience. It tolerates extreme heat, withstands cold down to -20°F (making it suitable for USDA Zone 5), and survives extended drought without supplemental water once established. It essentially takes care of itself.

Hummingbirds — particularly broad-tailed hummingbirds, Costa's hummingbirds, and Anna's hummingbirds — rely heavily on claret cup nectar during spring migration. A mature clump in full bloom will have hummingbirds visiting throughout the day from March through June.

"Claret cup is one of those plants that looks like it requires a special touch but actually thrives on neglect. The worst thing you can do is over-water it."

— Southwest native plant specialist

Soil and Drainage Requirements

Drainage is everything with claret cup cactus. In nature, these plants grow in decomposed granite, rocky limestone, or sandy loam — anywhere water moves through quickly. Standing water, even briefly, causes root rot that kills the plant within days.

In the garden:

  • Amend clay soil with at least 50% coarse decomposed granite or pumice before planting
  • Raise planting areas 6–12 inches above the surrounding grade in heavy soils
  • Avoid planting at the base of slopes where water pools after rain

A quality cactus and succulent potting mix works well for containers; cut it 50/50 with coarse perlite for extra drainage. If you're starting from scratch and wondering whether your soil can support these plants, the guide to starting a desert garden covers soil preparation in depth.

Planting Claret Cup Cactus

When to Plant

Fall planting (September–November) is ideal in the Sonoran Desert, giving roots time to establish before summer heat. Spring planting also works in most desert regions as long as you can water regularly through the first summer. For timing specific to your area, the post on the best time to plant in the desert covers seasonal windows across all desert zones.

Spacing and Placement

Plant claret cup in full sun — at least six hours of direct sun daily, with eight or more preferred. It will tolerate partial shade but blooms far less and grows more slowly. Space individual plants 18–24 inches apart; they'll expand outward over time and eventually merge into a dramatic clump.

Ideal placement includes:

  • South- or west-facing slopes for maximum sun and heat exposure
  • Alongside boulders or rocks that radiate warmth
  • Near the front of planting beds where the spring bloom show is fully visible

Avoid planting near walkways or high-traffic areas — the spines are sharp and will snag clothing and skin.

Planting Steps

  1. Dig a hole twice as wide as the root ball but no deeper
  2. Mix native soil with decomposed granite at a 1:1 ratio
  3. Set the plant slightly above grade to allow for settling
  4. Backfill and gently firm the soil around the base
  5. Apply a 2–3 inch layer of decomposed granite mulch around the plant, keeping it away from the stems

Watering After Planting

New transplants need regular watering to establish, but the goal is to taper off quickly. For the first two to three months, water deeply once every 10–14 days in summer, once a month in fall and spring, and not at all in winter unless the weather is exceptionally dry. After the first year, established claret cup cactus rarely needs supplemental water in the Sonoran or Chihuahuan Desert.

In the Mojave, a deep soak every month during summer (June–August) helps maintain vigor and bloom production. For broader guidance, the post on watering cacti without overwatering them covers the key signs to watch for.

Care and Maintenance

Fertilizing

Skip it. Claret cup cactus evolved in nutrient-poor rocky soils. Heavy fertilizing produces lush, weak growth prone to rot and pests. If plants seem sluggish after several years, one light application of balanced cactus fertilizer in early spring — once annually — is sufficient.

Cold Protection

Most E. triglochidiatus varieties need no cold protection in USDA Zones 5–11. In Zones 4 and below, cover plants with breathable frost cloth during hard freezes below -15°F.

Pests and Problems

Healthy claret cup cacti have few pest problems. Watch for:

  • Cochineal scale — white, cottony masses on the stems; remove with a strong jet of water or diluted isopropyl alcohol on a cotton swab
  • Root rot — soft, mushy base tissue from overwatering; prevention through proper drainage is the only real cure
  • Bacterial necrosis — spreading soft tissue; remove affected stems immediately with sterilized shears and allow the cuts to dry completely

Propagating Claret Cup Cactus

Claret cup is one of the easiest cacti to propagate by stem division. In early spring or fall, use thick leather gloves or folded newspaper to grip a stem or small cluster, twist gently, and separate it from the parent plant. Allow the cut end to callous for a week in a dry, shaded spot before potting or planting in gritty soil. Most divisions root within four to six weeks.

You can also grow claret cup from seed, though it takes three to five years to reach flowering size. Fresh seed has high germination rates when surface-sown on moist cactus mix in a warm location with bright indirect light.

Companion Plants for Claret Cup

Claret cup looks spectacular paired with plants that bloom or perform in the same season:

  • Desert marigold (Baileya multiradiata) — sunny yellow flowers contrast beautifully with scarlet claret cup blooms
  • Brittlebush (Encelia farinosa) — silver foliage and yellow flowers frame the cactus clump through winter and spring
  • Agave species — provide dramatic year-round architectural structure behind or alongside claret cup clumps
  • Apache plume (Fallugia paradoxa) — feathery seed heads and white blooms provide textural contrast through summer

For more on pairing desert plants for seasonal interest, the post on desert companion planting covers specific combinations across each desert region.

Where to Find Claret Cup Cactus

Look for claret cup at native plant nurseries, specialty cactus nurseries, and desert botanical garden plant sales in spring and fall. Wild-collected plants are legally protected in most states — always purchase nursery-propagated specimens. Online sources occasionally ship bare-root or container-grown plants; look for sellers who specify the variety and propagation method.

How to Grow Wolfberry in the Desert

August 2026  ·  7 min read

Photo by Magda Ehlers on Pexels

Walk a desert wash in autumn and you'll spot them: clusters of small, brilliant-red berries hanging from a thorny, arching shrub. That plant is wolfberry — one of the most underappreciated natives in the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts. Indigenous peoples across the Southwest have harvested and eaten wolfberries for thousands of years. Today, they belong in more desert gardens.

Wolfberries are part of the Lycium genus, which includes dozens of species native to arid and semi-arid regions worldwide. In the American Southwest, three species are most relevant to home gardeners: Lycium andersonii (Anderson's wolfberry or water jacket), Lycium fremontii (Fremont's wolfberry or desert thorn), and Lycium berlandieri (Berlandier's wolfberry). All three produce edible red to orange-red berries, provide excellent wildlife habitat, and ask almost nothing from you once established.

What Is Wolfberry?

Lycium species are dense, thorny deciduous-to-semi-evergreen shrubs that typically grow 3–8 feet tall and wide, depending on water availability and species. Their small, gray-green fleshy leaves are adapted to minimize water loss. In late winter and early spring, the plants flush out with tiny lavender or white tubular flowers that attract early-season native bees, hummingbirds, and butterflies. By late spring through summer, the flowers give way to the small (½-inch) round berries.

The berries are edible fresh or dried, with a flavor that ranges from mildly sweet to slightly tangy. If that flavor profile sounds familiar, it should — the commercial "goji berry" sold in health food stores is a distant relative (Lycium barbarum and L. chinense), native to China. Southwestern wolfberries offer a very similar nutritional profile and can be prepared the same way: eaten fresh, dried for trail mix, brewed into tea, or cooked into sauces and jams.

"Wolfberry is one of the desert's most generous plants — it feeds the birds, feeds the insects, feeds you, and asks for almost nothing in return. Plant one and step back."

Choosing the Right Species

All three Southwestern wolfberries are excellent garden plants, but species selection matters for your specific climate:

SpeciesHeightRangeCold Hardiness
Lycium andersonii4–8 ftSonoran, MojaveUSDA Zone 7 (0°F)
Lycium fremontii3–6 ftSonoran, lower ChihuahuanUSDA Zone 8 (10°F)
Lycium berlandieri4–7 ftChihuahuan, TexasUSDA Zone 6 (−10°F)

For Tucson and Phoenix gardeners, L. fremontii and L. andersonii are the most commonly available and most at home in Sonoran Desert conditions. In the Chihuahuan Desert (Las Cruces, El Paso, the Texas Big Bend region), L. berlandieri is the local native and the most cold-tolerant option.

Where to Plant Wolfberry

Site wolfberry in full sun. These plants are edge-of-wash and rocky slope species in the wild — they want maximum light and excellent drainage. Avoid low spots where water ponds after rain, as waterlogged roots will rot quickly.

Wolfberry pairs well with other native shrubs. In the Sonoran Desert, it grows naturally alongside desert lavender (Hyptis emoryi), brittlebush (Encelia farinosa), and jojoba (Simmondsia chinensis) — all plants that share its water and light requirements. The thorny nature of wolfberry also makes it useful as an informal barrier hedge or wildlife refuge planting. Cactus wrens, thrashers, and mockingbirds nest in the dense, spiny interior — protection from predators is built into the plant's structure.

Soil and Planting

Wolfberry thrives in the rocky, alkaline, nutrient-poor soils that discourage most cultivated plants. Native desert soils — sandy loam, caliche-underlain clay, decomposed granite — are ideal. Amending with compost or fertilizer is counterproductive: it encourages fast, weak growth and reduces drought tolerance.

The best planting windows are fall (September–November) and early spring (February–March). Fall planting is ideal in the Sonoran Desert — roots establish through the mild winter before summer heat arrives. For more on timing your desert plantings, see our seasonal guide to when to plant in the desert.

Dig a hole no deeper than the root ball but two to three times as wide. Set the plant so the root crown sits at or slightly above grade — never below it. Backfill with native soil only. Water deeply at planting, then follow the establishment watering schedule below.

Sourcing Wolfberry Plants

Wolfberry is available at most native plant nurseries in the Southwest. Look for Lycium species sold as small 1-gallon containers — these establish faster than larger specimens because their root systems haven't been confined. If you can't find plants locally, seeds are available online; they germinate well when cold-stratified for 30 days and then sown in a warm (75–85°F) spot.

Watering During Establishment

For the first summer after planting, water every 7–10 days deeply — enough to wet the soil 18 inches down. After the first full year, established wolfberry requires no supplemental irrigation in most Sonoran Desert locations where annual rainfall exceeds 10 inches. In the Mojave or in very dry years, one deep watering per month in summer is sufficient.

Overwatering is the single most common mistake with wolfberry. The plants respond to excess moisture with rank, floppy growth, reduced fruit production, and eventual root rot. Care and Maintenance

Pruning

Wolfberry benefits from a light annual pruning in late winter, just before new growth emerges. Remove dead wood, crossing branches, and any growth that has become too rangy. Wolfberry blooms on new growth, so pruning stimulates more flowers and heavier berry production. Use sharp bypass pruners and wear thick gloves — the thorns are serious.

Do not shear wolfberry into a formal shape. It looks best and fruits most heavily when allowed to grow in its natural arching, irregular form.

Fertilizing

Don't. Wolfberry evolved in nutrient-poor desert soils and does not benefit from fertilizer. Applications of nitrogen encourage excessive vegetative growth at the expense of flowers and fruit.

Pests and Problems

Wolfberry is remarkably pest-resistant in its native range. Occasional aphid infestations appear on new spring growth but are controlled quickly by the predatory wasps and lacewings that appear on wolfberry's flowers. Root rot from overwatering or poor drainage is the most common serious problem.

Harvesting and Using Wolfberries

Berries ripen from late spring through midsummer, turning from yellow-orange to bright red when fully ripe. Ripe berries drop easily from the branch — a gentle shake over a bucket is the most efficient harvest method. Eat them fresh within a day or two, or spread them on a screen to dry in the shade for a week.

Dried wolfberries can be used exactly like commercial goji berries: in granola, trail mix, oatmeal, or steeped in hot water for a mild, vitamin-rich tea. The berries are rich in carotenoids, vitamin C, and antioxidants — the same compounds that have made commercial goji berries a popular superfood. A basic food dehydrator or a simple screen-drying setup works perfectly.

Wildlife Value

Few native desert shrubs support as broad a range of wildlife as wolfberry. The flowers feed specialist native bees (including several Diadasia cactus bee species), hummingbirds, and sphinx moths. The berries are eaten by cactus wrens, curve-billed thrashers, mockingbirds, phainopeplas, and quail. Coyotes and foxes eat fallen berries as well. The thorny interior provides nesting habitat and predator protection for small birds. A single mature wolfberry bush can support dozens of wildlife interactions per day during its peak season.

If you're building a backyard wildlife habitat, wolfberry works well alongside desert companion plantings that support the full food web. The combination of wolfberry, saguaro, palo verde, and brittlebush is essentially a miniature Sonoran Desert in a residential yard.

Wolfberry vs. Commercial Goji Berry

Many desert gardeners ask whether they should grow commercial goji berry (Lycium barbarum) instead of native species. The answer is almost always native first. Commercial goji grows well in the desert but requires more water and care than natives, can spread aggressively in moist conditions, and does not support the same specialist native bee species that co-evolved with Southwestern Lycium. Nutritional differences between the species are negligible. Grow natives; eat the berries; support the bees.

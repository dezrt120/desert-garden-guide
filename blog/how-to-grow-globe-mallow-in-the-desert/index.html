How to Grow Globe Mallow in the Desert

July 2026  ·  5 min read

Photo by RDNE Stock project on Pexels

Globe mallow is one of the Southwest's most underappreciated native wildflowers. Sphaeralcea ambigua (desert globe mallow) and its close cousins blanket roadsides, washes, and rocky slopes across the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts in vivid shades of orange, apricot, pink, and lavender from late winter through early summer — and often again after monsoon rains. Despite its showiness, globe mallow is virtually unknown at garden centers compared to brittlebush or penstemon. That's a missed opportunity, because it's one of the most reliable, low-maintenance wildflowers you can plant in an arid garden.

What Is Globe Mallow?

Globe mallow belongs to the mallow family (Malvaceae), which also includes hibiscus, cotton, and hollyhock. Several species grow wild across the desert Southwest:

  • Desert globe mallow (Sphaeralcea ambigua) — the most common Sonoran species, reaching 2–4 feet with classic orange blooms
  • Scarlet globe mallow (S. coccinea) — a lower-growing species with deeper red-orange flowers, common on the Great Plains and northern Chihuahuan desert
  • Coulter's globe mallow (S. coulteri) — native to sandy desert washes in the lower Sonoran and Baja California
  • Pale globe mallow (S. incana) — produces soft gray-green foliage and pale pink to lavender flowers; a dramatic alternative to the standard orange

All species are characterized by their fuzzy, silvery-gray leaves, cup-shaped flowers arranged in tall spikes, and extraordinary drought tolerance. The hairy foliage can cause mild skin irritation in some people — wear protective garden gloves when pruning or handling stems.

Growing Conditions

Globe mallow evolved in some of the harshest terrain in North America, so it thrives in conditions that most ornamentals would refuse:

Sun and Soil

Full sun is non-negotiable. Globe mallow grown in partial shade gets leggy and blooms poorly. It prefers poor, fast-draining, alkaline desert soil — the kind that discourages most garden plants. Sandy loam, decomposed granite, and rocky fill all work perfectly. Avoid heavily amended beds with high organic matter, which encourages the root rot that kills globe mallow.

Water

Once established (typically after one summer growing season), globe mallow survives on natural rainfall alone in most of the Sonoran and Mojave deserts. During establishment, water every 10–14 days from spring through summer. After that, supplemental irrigation is optional — a deep soak once a month during dry stretches pushes heavier blooms, but the plant doesn't need it to survive. For comparison with how other drought-tolerant natives are watered, see our guide on how to water cacti and succulents without killing them — the deep-and-infrequent principle applies here too.

Cold Hardiness

Desert globe mallow is hardy to around 15°F, placing it in USDA zones 7b–11. Most of the Sonoran, Mojave, and lower Chihuahuan deserts fall well within that range, and no frost protection is needed for established plants.

Globe mallow is the wildflower equivalent of a native bee magnet — it hosts the specialist globe mallow bee (Diadasia spp.), which collects pollen from almost nothing else. Without globe mallow in the landscape, these solitary bees cannot complete their life cycle.

How to Plant Globe Mallow

From Seed

Globe mallow grows readily from seed, and direct seeding in fall or early spring mimics its natural germination cycle. Scatter seeds on bare, lightly raked soil in a sunny spot after your last hard freeze — late February through early March works across the Sonoran Desert. Press seeds gently into the soil surface; they need light to germinate and should not be buried more than ⅛ inch deep. Water lightly every few days until seedlings emerge (typically 10–21 days), then thin to 18–24 inches apart once plants reach 2 inches tall.

Globe mallow seed packets are available online — look for species-specific seeds rather than generic wildflower mixes for predictable color and habit.

From Container Plants

Nursery-grown transplants from a 1- or 4-gallon container establish faster than direct seed. Plant in fall (September–November) or early spring (February–March) to give roots time to settle before summer heat arrives. See our full guide on the best time of year to plant in the desert for the complete seasonal planting calendar.

Dig the hole twice as wide as the root ball but no deeper. Set the crown at or slightly above grade to prevent rot in alkaline desert soil. Backfill with native soil only — no amendments — and water deeply at planting.

Care and Maintenance

Pruning

Globe mallow naturally looks ragged by mid-summer heat and may die back to woody crowns by late July. Cut plants back by one-third to one-half after the main spring bloom fades — this triggers reblooming when monsoon rains arrive and can extend flowering into fall. A hard pruning every 2–3 years (cutting to 6 inches above the crown in late winter) keeps plants compact and prevents them from going open and woody at the base.

Mulching

A light layer of decomposed granite or pea gravel around the base reflects heat, retains soil moisture, and keeps the crown dry — exactly what globe mallow prefers. Avoid piling organic mulch against the stem. For more on mulching desert plants effectively, see our desert mulching guide.

Fertilizer

None needed. Globe mallow on a lean desert diet blooms more prolifically than plants grown in rich, amended soil. Added nitrogen pushes leafy growth at the direct expense of flowers.

Companion Planting

Globe mallow's soft orange blooms pair naturally with the desert's own color palette. In Sonoran gardens, it works beautifully planted alongside:

  • Penstemon (Penstemon parryi) — magenta tubes bloom simultaneously in spring, creating a vibrant color contrast
  • Desert marigold (Baileya multiradiata) — yellow daisies and orange globe mallow echo each other all summer and into fall
  • Brittlebush (Encelia farinosa) — silvery foliage and yellow blooms complement globe mallow's gray-green leaves perfectly
  • Blackfoot daisy (Melampodium leucanthum) — white flowers fill the gaps between globe mallow's orange spikes and bloom almost year-round

Wildlife Value

Few native plants deliver more wildlife value per gallon of water than globe mallow. It is the primary host plant for globe mallow bees (Diadasia spp.) — solitary native bees so specialized they collect pollen from almost no other plant genus. Without globe mallow in the landscape, these bees literally cannot reproduce successfully.

Beyond specialist bees, globe mallow attracts painted lady butterflies (which lay eggs on its leaves), Costa's and Anna's hummingbirds (especially to deep-orange-flowered forms), and white-winged doves that feed on the seeds. The fuzzy seed capsules also provide nesting material for several native cavity-nesting birds.

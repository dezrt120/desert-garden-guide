How to Grow Agastache (Hummingbird Mint) in the Desert

August 2026  ·  6 min read

Photo by Frank Cone on Pexels

Most flowering perennials that gardeners love — lavender, salvia, echinacea — struggle when summer temperatures climb past 105°F and the soil bakes dry for weeks. Agastache is the exception. Several species in this genus of flowering herbs are native to the Chihuahuan and Sonoran deserts, and they perform best exactly where other plants give up: full Sonoran sun, alkaline caliche soil, and monsoon cycles of feast and drought.

The result is a plant that blooms from midsummer through fall — peak hummingbird season — producing dense spikes of tubular flowers in orange, coral, pink, and purple. It's one of the most wildlife-dense perennials you can grow in the Southwest, and once established it asks almost nothing in return.

The Best Agastache Species for Desert Gardens

Not all agastache is created equal for desert conditions. Focus on the native and near-native species that evolved in arid climates.

Agastache rupestris — Sunset Hyssop

Native to rocky canyons in New Mexico and Arizona, sunset hyssop is arguably the toughest desert agastache available. It grows 2–3 feet tall and produces orange-pink flower spikes with a distinct root beer scent from July through October. Hardy to zone 5, it handles the full Sonoran Desert summer with reflected wall heat, thriving where most herbs would collapse. Buy Agastache rupestris seeds to start a colony from scratch.

Agastache cana — Texas Hummingbird Mint

Native to the Chihuahuan Desert in far western Texas and southern New Mexico, A. cana produces bold rose-pink flower spikes from July through frost. It grows 3–4 feet tall, carries a strong anise-rose fragrance, and is one of the most consistent hummingbird magnets in the Southwest. Anna's hummingbirds feed on it from first bloom; by August, Costa's hummingbirds are competing for the same stems.

Agastache aurantiaca — Orange Agastache

More compact at 18–24 inches, orange agastache produces peachy-orange flowers from early summer onward and fits easily in containers or front-of-border placements. Cultivars like 'Apricot Sprite' and 'Tango' are notably heat-tolerant and perform well in both low Sonoran and mid-elevation Chihuahuan conditions.

Named Cultivars Worth Trying

Breeders have crossed native species into dozens of named cultivars — 'Desert Sunrise', 'Kudos Coral', 'Blue Fortune' — many of which handle heat above 100°F reliably. When choosing cultivars at a nursery, look specifically for those labeled heat-tolerant to zone 10 or those bred from A. rupestris or A. cana parents.

Soil and Drainage: The Non-Negotiable

Agastache will not survive in dense, waterlogged soil. Like most members of the mint family (Lamiaceae), it demands fast drainage above all else. In Sonoran and Chihuahuan desert gardens, two obstacles are common:

  • Caliche hardpan — A calcite layer even 18 inches down can cause water to pool at the root zone after monsoon rains. See our guide to dealing with caliche soil for how to identify and break through it before planting.
  • Heavy clay or compacted fill — Mix in 30–40% coarse pumice or horticultural perlite to open the texture and prevent root rot.

A raised border or gentle slope where monsoon runoff drains away naturally is the ideal setting. Apply a 2–3 inch layer of decomposed granite as a surface mulch — it wicks moisture away from the crown between rains and mimics the rocky scree where these plants evolved.

When to Plant

In the low Sonoran Desert (Phoenix metro, Tucson, Yuma), fall planting from mid-September through November gives agastache time to establish a root system through the mild winter. Plants grown this way emerge with strong, established crowns when summer heat arrives and bloom with full vigor in their first year.

In the Chihuahuan Desert (El Paso, Las Cruces, higher-elevation Arizona), where winters are colder, a spring planting 4–6 weeks before peak summer heat is more reliable. For the complete timing logic across all desert regions, see The Best Time of Year to Plant in the Desert.

How to Water Agastache in the Desert

Once established, desert-native agastache species tolerate genuine drought. The watering rhythm mirrors the principle behind watering cacti and succulents: water deeply, then let the soil dry out before watering again.

"Desert agastache actually blooms harder under mild drought stress. Overwatered plants produce lush, floppy stems and fewer flowers — the opposite of what you want."

Growth stageWatering frequency
First 4 weeks after plantingEvery 2–3 days
Establishment (weeks 5–12)Weekly, soaking 12–18 inches deep
Established, summer peakEvery 10–14 days
Established, fall and winterEvery 3–4 weeks

Drip irrigation placed 6–8 inches from the crown delivers water where roots forage without wetting the stem base — the most common source of crown rot in desert agastache.

Sun, Placement, and Microclimates

Agastache demands full sun — at least 6 hours, and it handles 8–10 hours without complaint. In the Sonoran Desert, it thrives in full western exposure including reflected heat from stucco walls that would scorch many other plants. Avoid low-lying spots where cold air pools in winter or where monsoon runoff collects after heavy rain.

In the Mojave Desert, some afternoon shade from a nurse tree or shade canopy during the peak 2–5 pm heat window can extend the bloom season and reduce water demand in climates with less monsoon humidity than the Sonoran.

Companion Planting

Agastache pairs naturally with other late-summer bloomers in the desert plant community. Strong combinations include:

  • Salvia greggii (autumn sage) — blooms at the same time, attracts the same pollinators, and tolerates identical watering schedules
  • Penstemon parryi (Parry's penstemon) — a spring-blooming tubular flower that extends the hummingbird season from March through October when combined with agastache
  • Leucophyllum frutescens (Texas sage) — a moisture-indicator shrub that erupts in purple bloom after monsoon events, harmonizing with agastache's peak flowering period
  • Gaillardia aristata (blanket flower) — gold-and-orange daisy that fills the front of a border while agastache anchors the middle tier

For the principles behind building thriving plant communities like this, see Desert Companion Planting: What Grows Well Together.

Pruning and Winter Care

Cut stems back by one-third in late October or early November after bloom winds down. This prevents wind rock in winter storms and encourages a denser flush of new growth the following spring. Leave some stem length — agastache's hollow stems provide overwintering habitat for native cavity-nesting bees, especially small sweat bees in the genus Halictus.

In the low Sonoran Desert, established A. rupestris and A. cana are root-hardy and regrow reliably from the crown after frost kills the tops. In the Mojave or high-elevation Chihuahuan Desert (above 4,000 feet), mulch the crown with 3–4 inches of coarse gravel after the first frost to protect it through extended cold.

Propagation

Agastache is easy to multiply. Scatter fresh seed directly on fast-draining prepared soil in fall — seeds need the cool desert winter to stratify and germinate in spring. For stem cuttings, take 4-inch tips in early summer before flower spikes emerge, treat with rooting hormone powder, and pot in a 50/50 mix of perlite and potting soil. Roots form in 3–5 weeks in warm conditions.

Established clumps can also be divided in early spring — dig the crown, separate sections with a sharp spade, and replant immediately with a generous initial watering.

Wildlife Value

Few plants deliver as much wildlife benefit per square foot in the desert Southwest. Agastache draws:

  • Anna's and Costa's hummingbirds — reliable visitors from July through first frost
  • Monarch butterflies — a nectar stopover during fall migration through the Chihuahuan corridor
  • Native bumblebees — Bombus sonorus and B. pensylvanicus are highly attracted to the tubular flowers
  • Sphinx moths — active at dusk on A. cana, which appears to have co-evolved with these pollinators
  • Specialist native bees — several Halictus and Agapostemon species collect pollen almost exclusively from agastache relatives

Planted in a mass of three or more plants, agastache becomes a visible migration waystation and a humming center of pollinator activity from late July through October — one of the desert garden's most dynamic seasonal performances.

How to Grow Penstemon in the Desert: Native Beauty for Pollinators

August 2026  ·  6 min read

Photo by David Desrocher on Pexels

Every spring in the Sonoran Desert, something remarkable happens: coral-red tubes of Penstemon parryi shoot up from seemingly barren ground, drawing a constant stream of hummingbirds and native bees to their blooms. Within weeks, those same stalks set seed and the plants go dormant — having survived summer on little more than rainfall and stubbornness. This is penstemon in its element. Few native wildflowers deliver as much visual impact with as little input, and for desert gardeners, they represent one of the smartest investments you can make in your landscape.

With over 270 species found across North America — and dozens native to the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts — penstemon (commonly called beardtongue) is one of the most diverse native wildflower genera on the continent. Learning which species grow naturally in your region, and giving them the right conditions, is the key to success.

What Is Penstemon (Beardtongue)?

Penstemons are flowering perennials in the family Plantaginaceae. Their tubular, two-lipped flowers bloom in shades of scarlet, coral, lavender, deep purple, white, and pale pink depending on the species. The common name "beardtongue" refers to the sterile fifth stamen, which often bears a tuft of hairs. While some non-native cultivars are bred for showy blooms, the native desert species evolved alongside local hummingbirds and native bees — making them far more ecologically valuable and, crucially, far better adapted to arid conditions.

Most desert penstemons are short-lived perennials or subshrubs, lasting three to five years but reseeding readily. In a well-established planting, you'll rarely need to replant — they self-sow to fill gaps on their own.

Best Penstemon Species for Desert Gardens

Choosing species native to your specific desert is essential. A Mojave native may struggle in the Chihuahuan, and vice versa. Here are four standout species for the three major southwestern desert regions.

Penstemon parryi — Parry's Penstemon (Sonoran Desert)

The most iconic penstemon of the Sonoran Desert, Penstemon parryi produces tall, arching spikes up to 5 feet covered in bright coral-pink to deep rose flowers. It blooms February through April, making it one of the earliest spring wildflowers in the Phoenix and Tucson regions. Plant it in full sun and mix it with other Sonoran wildflower seeds for a stunning naturalistic display. Parry's penstemon is a magnet for Costa's hummingbirds, which time their migration to its bloom.

Penstemon pseudospectabilis — Desert Penstemon (Sonoran/Chihuahuan)

Penstemon pseudospectabilis grows to about 3 feet and produces stunning magenta-pink flowers with distinctive clasping, blue-green foliage. It thrives in rocky slopes and canyon walls across southern Arizona and New Mexico. Its bloom period — March through May — overlaps with peak hummingbird activity. This species handles caliche soils better than most, making it ideal for gardens dealing with that stubborn hardpan. The blue-gray foliage is attractive even out of bloom.

Penstemon eatonii — Firecracker Penstemon (Mojave/Great Basin)

If you garden in the Mojave or high desert of Nevada and Utah, Penstemon eatonii is your penstemon. Its long, bright scarlet tubes — nearly perfectly cylindrical — are designed for hummingbirds and bloom from February through April. It's one of the most cold-hardy desert penstemons, tolerating frost well below 0°F when dormant. In the Las Vegas basin and Mojave foothills, it's a reliable performer in decomposed granite and rocky soils.

Penstemon superbus — Superb Penstemon (Chihuahuan Desert)

True to its name, Penstemon superbus is spectacular — growing 4 to 6 feet tall with coral-red flowers that bloom from March through June. Native to the Chihuahuan Desert of New Mexico, Texas, and northern Mexico, it's among the largest penstemon species in the Southwest. It establishes well from seed and naturalizes freely in El Paso, southern New Mexico, and far west Texas landscapes. Pair it with Chihuahuan Desert native seed mixes for a cohesive planting.

When and How to Plant Penstemon

Like most desert natives, penstemon establishes best when it can take advantage of cooler temperatures and winter rainfall. The fall planting window — October through November — is ideal across all three desert regions. This gives roots time to establish before spring blooms and summer heat.

You have two options: seed or transplant.

From seed: Direct-sow in fall, scattering seeds on the soil surface (penstemon needs light to germinate — don't bury them). Lightly rake into the top ⅛ inch of soil. Cold stratification occurs naturally over winter in most desert regions. Germination happens in late winter as temperatures warm.

From transplants: Container-grown plants give you a head start but need careful planting. Dig a hole slightly wider than the root ball, amend minimally (these plants don't want rich soil), and plant at the same depth as the nursery container. Water in thoroughly and then step back — over-attentiveness is penstemon's main enemy.

Watering Penstemon in the Desert

The number-one mistake desert gardeners make with penstemon is overwatering. These plants are adapted to bone-dry summers. Once established — typically after one full growing season — most desert penstemon species need zero supplemental irrigation if they receive 8 inches or more of annual rainfall.

During establishment (the first six to twelve months), water every seven to ten days from October through April, and reduce to once every two to three weeks in summer if temperatures regularly exceed 105°F. After that first season, you can typically let winter rains do the work entirely.

Signs of overwatering include yellowing lower leaves, stem rot at the crown, and plants that look lush but collapse suddenly in summer. For guidance on reading your plants' water needs, see our deep dive on watering drought-adapted desert plants — many of the same principles apply to penstemon.

If you're growing penstemon in containers, use a fast-draining cactus and succulent potting mix and make sure the pot has large drainage holes. Container-grown penstemon needs more water than in-ground plants but is still far more drought-tolerant than typical ornamentals.

Soil, Sun, and Site Selection

All desert penstemons share a few non-negotiables:

  • Full sun — at least six to eight hours of direct sunlight per day. Shade causes leggy, weak growth and poor flowering.
  • Excellent drainage — penstemon crowns rot in standing water. Rocky slopes, decomposed granite mulch, and raised berms all work well. Avoid heavy clay without significant amendment.
  • Lean soil — resist the urge to fertilize. Rich, amended soil produces lush foliage and almost no flowers. Native penstemons evolved in nutrient-poor desert soils; that's where they perform best.

Siting on a slope or against a south- or west-facing slope mimics their natural rocky canyon habitat and improves drainage passively. Decomposed granite as a top dressing keeps crowns dry and dramatically reduces rot risk.

Pruning and Deadheading

After blooms fade, you face a choice: deadhead to prevent self-sowing, or leave seed stalks to naturalize. In most desert gardens, leaving some seed heads is the right call — penstemon self-sows reliably and the seedlings transplant well when small. If you want to control spread, deadhead about half the stalks and leave the rest.

Cut old flowering stalks to the basal rosette in late spring after seeds have dropped. The foliage clump typically stays green through summer in cooler elevations but may go fully dormant at lower desert elevations — don't panic and pull the plant. New growth emerges in fall.

Penstemon for Desert Pollinators

Few plant groups are as important to desert pollinator communities as penstemon. The tubular, nectar-rich flowers are perfectly shaped for hummingbird feeding — and the length of the tube often excludes less-efficient pollinators, concentrating visits from the most effective ones. Costa's hummingbirds (Calypte costae) in the Sonoran Desert time their spring migration almost exactly to coincide with Penstemon parryi bloom. Anna's hummingbirds work P. eatonii in the Mojave through winter.

Beyond hummingbirds, native bumble bees and mason bees visit penstemon flowers for pollen. Some specialist native bees — including Osmia species — are documented to prefer penstemon over nearly every other native plant. Planting a diverse mix of penstemon species extends the bloom window and supports pollinators from February through early summer.

For a complete hummingbird-attracting strategy, penstemon pairs beautifully with other desert natives like desert salvia, Justicia, and Anisacanthus in a layered pollinator garden.
